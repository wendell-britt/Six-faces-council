## Anchor

A non-profit is a type of game (Wendell, pass 1). The artist corps trains and pays artists to make human-made work for
non-profits, and every engagement leaves an asset in a shared commons that the next one draws on.

## Shaman (58 Banter, becoming 34 Vigour)

**The reading says the exchange is the whole of it.** Phase 1 is one relationship between one artist and one
organisation, and its felt success is whether both would do it again. Vigour warns that force is available and
unaimed: a warm start has goodwill to spare, and goodwill spent without a scope becomes a favour.

**The risk is a favour dressed as a pilot.** In a warm start the org may expect a discount and the artist may give
one. Either one breaks `pos-ac-paid-hours`. The done list must name the payment and who funds it before work starts.

**The recommendation is an ending ritual.** Done includes a short closing conversation where the org and the artist
each say what they would change. Their words go in the write-up as they said them.

## Architect (59 Dispersal, becoming 44 Serendipity)

**The reading says what was held rigid is coming apart, and the warning is one piece of evidence.** Phase 1 is a single
case. It can prove the pipeline works once. It cannot prove demand, scale or a curriculum, and the done list says so,
so nobody builds on it as if it did.

**The structure of Phase 1 is five records**, each a file in the commons: the engagement agreement, the signed consent
form, the hour log, the commons asset with its provenance record, and the case write-up. Done is when all five exist
and agree with each other.

**On legibility, the Architect wants the plainest ledger that could work.** An hour log with four fields (date, person,
hours, category: client, commons, awareness, training) and the rate paid is enough for one artist. SourceCred's graph
and timebanking's credits both solve problems Phase 1 does not have yet.

## Challenger (3 Sprouting, becoming 8 Alliance)

**The reading says this is early, and the mess is not a verdict.** The Challenger's pass 1 dissent (economics before
structure) stands, and this pass honours it: T1 runs before any artist is recruited and before the pipeline grows past
its first stage.

**The anti-pattern is the self-funded proof.** If Wendell pays for the deliverable, Phase 1 proves he can buy art. Done
requires money from outside him: the org's own budget, a grant or a sponsor. A Wendell-funded run can still happen, but
it is recorded as a rehearsal and does not close Phase 1.

**The falsification tests:**

- **T1 gap.** If the human-only price is more than twice what the org spends today on the same kind of work, and no
  funder covers the difference by 2026-11-30, Phase 1 rescopes to a smaller deliverable before anyone is recruited.
- **Paid in full.** If the artist is not paid in full within 30 days of the org accepting the work, Phase 1 stops and
  the council runs a pass on why.
- **Commons reuse.** If the commons asset from Phase 1 cannot be named as reusable in a second engagement's scope, the
  commons engine (`pos-ac-commons-engine`) is unproven and Phase 2 waits on a pass.

**On SourceCred, the Challenger cites its end.** The SourceCred organisation dissolved in March 2022 when its grant
money ran out and community interest faded, after distributing over $2M across communities from 2019. Contributors
described scores-driven rewards as everyone rating each other, and loose platforms like Discord rewarded social
dynamics more than work ([MetaGame](https://metagame.substack.com/p/sourcecred-is-dead-long-live-sourcecred),
[Summer of Protocols](https://forum.summerofprotocols.com/t/pig-revisiting-the-source-improving-the-sourcecred-credit-attribution-protocol/446),
[Uniswap forum](https://gov.uniswap.org/t/diversity-for-working-groups/24097/3)). With one artist there is no graph to
score.

## Regent (51 Shock, becoming 34 Vigour)

**The reading says it went off and will go off again.** The shock in Phase 1 is legal: an artist paid as a contractor
who does commons work under the org's direction looks like an employee to most tests. The Regent names the risk and
does not settle it.

**Phase gates.** Phase 0 (now): the done list, the T1 design and the consent form exist. Phase 1 opens when the anchor
is named and T1 has its number. Phase 1 closes when every done item below is met. Phase 2 (a second artist or a second
org) opens only after Phase 1 closes and a pass reads the write-up.

**Non-negotiables:** every hour paid; outside money; signed consent before work starts; a lawyer reads the contractor
agreement before the first signature.

**The legal risk, flagged for a lawyer.** Worker classification is decided by the IRS common-law test, the Department of
Labor's economic-reality test and, for Oregon, the state's own tests for unemployment insurance and workers'
compensation. Commons work that the corps directs, on its schedule, using its tools, points toward employee status.
Paying the same person as a contractor for client work and for commons work may also blur the line. The lawyer's
questions: can commons and awareness hours be contracted at all, or must they be wages; does the cooperative-member
stage change that; and what does Oregon require. The council does not answer these.

## The Phase 1 definition-of-done (T2)

**Scope:** one artist, one non-profit from Wendell's network (`q-ac-first-anchor` A), one funded deliverable, fully
documented.

**Done when every item is true:**

1. The anchor non-profit is named and T1 has priced the deliverable, with the gap in dollars written down.
2. An engagement agreement is signed: scope, price, dates, and the commons terms (an interim clause until T3's license
   exists).
3. The consent form is signed by the org before work starts.
4. The money comes from outside Wendell: the org's budget, a grant or a sponsor, named in the agreement.
5. A lawyer has read the contractor agreement before the artist signs it.
6. The deliverable is delivered and the org accepts it in writing.
7. The artist is paid in full for every logged hour (client, commons, awareness, training) within 30 days of
   acceptance.
8. At least one asset from the work is in the commons with its provenance record and license terms.
9. A closing conversation is held, and the case write-up records the cost, the T1 gap against the actual, what the org
   and the artist said they would change (their words), and what the case does not prove.

**What done proves:** one human-made deliverable can be priced, funded from outside, delivered, paid at a fair rate,
and leave a reusable asset. **What it does not prove:** demand beyond one org, recruitment, a curriculum, or a
grantmaker's interest (a warm start may be discounted, as pass 1 said).

**What stops it:** the three Challenger falsifiers above, the org withdrawing consent, or the lawyer finding the
contractor structure unsafe. Any stop sends the strand to a pass before anything else happens. Nothing recruits before
item 1 is met.

## T1 design

**Inputs, from the anchor org:** one deliverable it needs in the next six months; what it spent in the last twelve
months on that kind of work (who, how much, any AI tools used); its budget line for it. Public numbers come from the
org's Form 990 on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer where it files one.

**Method:** scope the deliverable in hours by category (client, commons, awareness), price it at a named rate source
(the source is written down with the number), and add the commons share. **Output:** human-only price, current spend,
and the gap in dollars. **Pass:** the number exists by 2026-10-30, or the step's date moves with the reason. The
falsifier on the gap is the Challenger's first test.

## Paid-hours legibility

- **SourceCred: no.** It dissolved in 2022, and it needs a graph of many contributors to score. Its failure modes
  (rating each other, rewarding social noise) cut against `pos-ac-paid-hours`, which pays hours in money.
- **Timebanking: later.** Hour-for-hour credit fits mutual aid between artists. The IRS has said, in a private letter
  ruling that is not precedent, that a time-credit program with hour-for-hour value and no fees was not a barter
  exchange, and it declined to say whether members earn income
  ([Community Currencies Law](https://communitycurrencieslaw.org/choice-of-entity-and-tax-exemption/),
  [communitycurrencieslaw.org income tax](https://www.communitycurrencieslaw.org/income-tax)). Trading credits for
  services an artist normally sells likely counts as taxable barter, so credits can never stand in for pay. Portland
  already has one, [hOur Exchange](https://hourexchangeportland.org/faqs.htm), which a later pass could partner with
  rather than build.
- **Now:** the plain hour log, four categories, every hour paid in dollars.

## Contractor-to-cooperative pipeline

1. **Contractor.** Paid per engagement at a written rate for every hour. Moves on after two accepted engagements and a
   peer review by the artist's last client and one other artist.
2. **Associate.** Steady engagements, a vote on the commons' rules, still paid per hour. Moves on after six months and
   a vote of the existing members (or, while there are none, the founding circle).
3. **Member.** A share in the cooperative and a vote on everything.

Kept to circles only where needed (`pos-ac-minimal-holacracy`). The lawyer flag above applies to stages 1 and 2. Which
legal form the cooperative takes alongside a 501(c)(3) (`q-ac-standalone-or-inside` A) is a lawyer's question too.

## The consent form (one page)

1. Who made this: the artist's name, and that the work is human-made.
2. AI use, ticked by the client: none (the default); AI for admin only (scheduling, email); AI in drafts the artist
   then reworks; other, written in.
3. The commons: which assets from this work enter the commons, what the client may use, and what others may use.
4. Records: the client agrees that the hour log and the case write-up may be shared, with anything private removed.
5. Withdrawal: the client may withdraw AI consent at any time; work already done stays as agreed.
6. Signatures and date.
