## What the commons is

Every engagement leaves at least one asset that the next engagement can draw on: a layout library, a comms kit, a brand
system with the client's own marks taken out. The commons is the store of those assets and their provenance records.
Each asset has three parties: the **artist** who made it, the **client** non-profit it was made for, and the **corps**
(the 501(c)(3)) that holds the commons.

## 1. Ownership

1. **The artist keeps the copyright.** Under US law a contractor owns what they make unless they sign it away, and a
   contractor's work counts as "made for hire" only in nine narrow categories with a signed agreement (17 U.S.C. § 101).
   The corps does not ask for an assignment. Keeping the copyright with the artist fits the pipeline toward a cooperative
   the artists own.
2. **The client owns its own marks and content**: its name, logo, photographs of its people, its text and its data. These
   never enter the commons. What enters is the reusable part with the client's marks taken out.
3. **The corps holds a license, not the copyright.** It holds the commons in trust for the artists and member
   non-profits. If the corps dissolves, its licenses pass to another 501(c)(3) that keeps the commons open on the same
   terms, as the dissolution clause of a 501(c)(3) already requires for its assets.

## 2. Use rights

1. **The client** gets a perpetual, worldwide, non-exclusive, royalty-free license to use and adapt the delivered work
   for its own mission, including through its own staff and vendors. The client may not resell the work.
2. **The corps** gets a perpetual, non-exclusive license to keep the commons asset, adapt it, and sublicense it under
   section 2.3. The artist grants this license when the client accepts the work.
3. **Member non-profits** (any non-profit with an engagement through the corps) may use and adapt commons assets for
   their own mission work, at no charge, as long as the provenance record's credit line goes with the work.
4. **Nobody** may sell a commons asset, use it for a for-profit client, or use it to train or tune an AI model without
   the artist's written consent. Portland's allergy to AI makes this the default, and the consent form's AI box governs
   only how the work was made, not what is done with it afterwards.
5. **The artist** may reuse their own work anywhere, including in their portfolio and with other clients, once the
   client's marks are taken out.
6. **Licenses do not lapse.** An artist who leaves the corps keeps their copyright, and the licenses already given stay
   in force. An artist may ask for their name to be taken off a credit line; the provenance record keeps it privately.

## 3. Payment

1. **Every hour on a commons asset is paid** (`pos-ac-paid-hours`): logged in the hour log as category "commons" and
   paid in dollars at the engagement's rate, within 30 days of acceptance. The commons share is priced into the
   engagement (T1's method) and named in the agreement, so the client knows what it is paying for and why.
2. **Reuse pays the hours of reuse.** When a later engagement draws on a commons asset, the artist adapting it is paid
   for the adaptation hours at that engagement's rate, and the engagement's price shows the hours the commons saved.
   That saving is the commons engine working (`pos-ac-commons-engine`).
3. **No per-use royalties in Phase 1.** They need a ledger the plain hour log cannot carry (`pos-ac-legibility-later`).
   Whether the original artist earns a share when their asset is reused is a question for the Phase 2 pass, when there
   is a second engagement to measure.
4. **The license itself costs nothing** to the client or to member non-profits. Money moves for hours, never for
   permission.

## 4. The provenance record

One per asset, stored beside it in the commons. Fields: asset name; artist or artists; engagement and client; date
accepted; hours by category; the consent form's AI box as ticked; what earlier commons assets it drew on; this license's
version; the credit line ("Made by [artist] for [client] through the artist corps. Human-made." or, where the consent
form allowed AI, the box it ticked); every later engagement that reused it. The record names who made it, which is the
provenance frame in practice (`pos-ac-provenance-frame`).

## For the lawyer (test T4, due 2026-11-20)

Added to the three classification questions in the pass file:

1. Does a non-exclusive license from the artist, without assignment, let the corps sublicense to member non-profits
   with enough certainty, or does it need an assignment or an exclusive license to enforce section 2.4?
2. Is the AI-training bar in section 2.4 enforceable as a license term against a member non-profit, and against a
   third party who gets the work from one?
3. Does paying the artist for commons hours, under the corps' direction, change whether the copyright is theirs under
   section 1.1 if the lawyer finds those hours must be wages (work made by an employee in the scope of employment
   belongs to the employer)?

The third question matters most: if commons hours must be wages, the corps owns those assets by law and section 1.1
needs a written assignment back to the artist, or a different design.
